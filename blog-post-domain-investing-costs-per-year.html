What It Really Costs to Hold a Domain Portfolio for One Year

Most people who start buying domains plan for one cost: the price of registering the name. Then the first renewal email arrives, and domain investing costs per year become real. Renewals are the big one, but privacy, research tools and the fee you pay when a name sells all take something too.

Quick answer: For 50 .com names at a low-cost registrar, renewals alone come to roughly $560–$590 a year. Privacy is free at most registrars, tools range from free to paid subscriptions, and a marketplace commission is only charged when you sell.

The base price also went up. On November 1, 2026, Verisign, the company that runs .com, raised what it charges registrars to $10.97 a year. Add a $0.20 fee that goes to ICANN, the group that oversees domain names, and the lowest any registrar can charge for a .com is now about $11.17.

I’ll go through each cost of holding a portfolio for one year, then finish with a budget for 50 names. If you are asking how much it costs to own a domain portfolio, that budget near the end gives you a direct answer.

I usually give a name three years. If it still has not shown a good click-through rate by then, I drop it. That rule has cost me more than a little.

Domain renewal cost portfolio: the biggest line item

Renewals are the biggest cost for most portfolios, so start here.

Why .com renewals just got more expensive

Verisign announced the .com increase in April 2026: from $10.26 to $10.97 a year, starting November 1, 2026. It was the first rise since 2024. Under its contract, Verisign can raise the price by up to 7% a year for the rest of the current cycle. If it uses every increase, Domain Name Wire estimates the price would reach about $13.42 by the end of the cycle. It may not, but it is worth planning for.

.net is moving too. Verisign has announced a rise from $10.91 to $12.00 on March 1, 2027.

Renewals matter for new extensions too. Strong sign-ups with weak renewal rates are part of the story in The TLD Fight Is On: 1,615 Applications, and One Question Nobody Is Asking.

What registrars charge for a .com renewal

Each registrar adds its own margin on top of Verisign’s price. Cloudflare sells .com at cost, about $11.17. Dynadot and Porkbun are close, at roughly $11.60 and $11.80. Namecheap renewals are about $19.98, and GoDaddy about $22.99, both plus a $0.20 ICANN fee. Prices change often, so check the checkout page before you decide. For a closer look, see Domain Renewal Prices Compared.

Renewal traps to watch

  • First-year promos. A first year of a few dollars often becomes a renewal of $20 or more. Always read the renewal price before you register.
  • Mixed registrars. A portfolio spread over four accounts means four renewal calendars and easy-to-miss expiry emails.
  • Pre-paying years. Some investors renew several years ahead to lock today’s price. The trade-off is cash tied up in names you may drop later.

Domain privacy protection cost: usually free, sometimes not

WHOIS is the public record of who owns a domain. Privacy protection replaces your name, email and address in that record with the registrar’s details. Cloudflare, Porkbun, Namecheap and Dynadot all include it for free.

Not every registrar does. One 2026 comparison says GoDaddy charges $9.99 a year extra for privacy, while another says it is free on eligible domains. The sources disagree, so check your own account.

The cost adds up fast. At $9.99 a name, privacy on 50 domains is $499.50 a year, which nearly doubles a low renewal bill. If your registrar charges for it, moving the names may cost less than staying.

Two catches:

  • Some extensions do not support privacy at all, so “free privacy” usually means free on domains that qualify.
  • Privacy hides your details from the public. Your registrar still knows who you are, and it may have to share your details with a court or the authorities.

Domain investing tools I use: what’s free and what’s worth paying for

Tools are the part of the budget you control most. You can spend nothing or a lot, depending on how much research you do. Most setups cover four jobs:

  • Finding names. Lists of domains that are about to expire. Free versions exist; paid plans mostly add filters and alerts.
  • Checking real sale prices. Use a public database of reported sales. Look at what names actually sold for, not at asking prices, which are often much higher. I walk through this in Why This Domain Sits at $3,000–$4,500, Not $10,000.
  • Checking trademarks. The official trademark databases are free to search. A cheap name can turn expensive if it copies a brand. My free 15-minute trademark check shows how.
  • Selling. Most marketplaces are free to list on and take a cut when the name sells. Some sales-page services charge a monthly fee instead and no commission, apart from payment processing.

The one tool I can point to directly is my own free domain appraisal tool. You type a name and it gives a score from 0 to 100, based on length, extension, keywords, how easy the name is to say, and whether it has hyphens or digits. It also lists strengths and risks and suggests a plan to sell. It does not use real sales data, so I treat the score as a first filter, not a price.

I do not lean heavily on any tool. Different tools come and go, and each one is happy to tell you what to do next. I only trust a tool after I have tried it by hand and seen the result myself.

What selling costs you

Commissions are not a holding cost, but they decide how much of a sale you keep. Published rates vary a lot by platform and by how the sale is routed, from about 5% to 25%. Afternic’s own fee page lists a standard 25% commission with a $15 minimum, under rules from February 2023. Read the current fee page before you list.

On a $2,000 sale it looks like this:

For private deals, an escrow service holds the buyer’s money until the name is transferred. It charges a fee based on the sale amount, which buyer and seller can split. Check the provider’s own fee calculator before you agree on terms.

Costs beginners forget

Some costs sneak in. Depending on where you live, VAT, GST or sales tax may be added to registration fees, and paying in a foreign currency adds conversion and card charges. Missing a renewal can be expensive too: one registrar’s published pricing lists $120 to restore an expired .com. Profit from sales may also be taxable in your country, so ask a local tax professional. And a big portfolio takes time to review, even if that never shows up on a bill.

How to lower domain investing costs per year

  1. Compare renewal prices, not first-year prices.
  2. Choose a registrar with free privacy. That removes a whole cost line.
  3. Use fewer registrars. One or two accounts are easier to track than five.
  4. Review the portfolio once a year, before renewals are due. Drop names with no inquiries and no clear buyer.
  5. Set a reminder about 30 days before expiry for names you are unsure about.
  6. Check real sale prices in a sales database, so you do not keep paying every year for names priced far above the market.

Dropping names has its own price. One good sale in a year can make you forget a whole year of renewal bills. And a name you let go by mistake can bother you for a long time. Both are hard to explain to anyone who has not been through it.

For an example of a name priced too high that never sold, read Domain Autopsy: Why This “Premium” Listing Never Sold.

Final thoughts

Domain investing costs per year come down to four things: renewals, privacy, tools and selling fees. Renewals are the largest, and the registrar you pick can double that bill.

A good first step: list every name you own with its renewal date and price, then compare that list with the tables above to see where you are overpaying.

Frequently asked questions

How much does it cost to own a domain portfolio?

For 50 .com names, expect roughly $560 to $600 a year in renewals at a low-cost registrar, plus any paid tools. At a registrar with high renewals and paid privacy, the same portfolio can pass $1,600. Selling fees only apply when a name sells.

Is domain privacy protection worth paying for?

Privacy itself is worth having, but you rarely need to pay for it. Several major registrars include it free on eligible domains. If yours charges around $10 per name, the extra cost across a portfolio adds up fast.

Will .com renewal prices keep going up?

They can. Verisign may raise its price by up to 7% a year for the rest of its current contract cycle, but it does not have to. It is safer to budget for a small rise each year.

Can I lower my renewal costs by transferring domains?

Often yes. A standard transfer adds one year to the registration, and the transfer fee is usually close to the renewal price. A domain generally cannot be transferred within 60 days of registration or a previous transfer, so check the lock status first.

What happens if I miss a renewal?

Most registrars give a grace period, but terms differ. After that, restoring an expired name can cost far more than a normal renewal, and the name may drop into public auction or become available to anyone.
