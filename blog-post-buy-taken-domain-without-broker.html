How to Buy a Taken Domain Without a Broker (On a Small Budget)

You found the perfect name, but it’s already taken. If the price is in the hundreds of dollars, not the thousands, you can usually handle the purchase yourself. Here are the steps, a simple email you can copy, and the numbers.

Short answer: Yes, you can buy a taken domain without a broker. Find out who owns it, send a short and polite message, agree on a price in writing, and pay through escrow (a neutral third party that holds your money until the domain is yours). For names in the hundreds of dollars, that is usually all you need.

You type the name into a registrar (the company where you buy domains), and it tells you the name is taken. So you search for what to do next, and the first results are all domain brokers offering to “handle everything” for you.

It’s true that a broker can help. But you don’t always need one. This guide shows you how to buy a taken domain on your own, what to say to the owner, and how to avoid losing money along the way.

Do you really need a domain broker?

A broker contacts the owner for you, negotiates the price and helps with the paperwork. That can be a real help. But it costs money, and it isn’t always quick.

GoDaddy’s guide to buying a domain someone else owns says broker commissions are typically 10–20%. Dynadot’s service works a little differently: you pay a $19.99 fee up front that is not refundable, plus 10% of the final price if the deal goes through, with a $50 minimum. Wix adds that up-front fees are usually kept even when the owner says no.

Wix also makes a simple point that’s easy to miss. If a domain already has a fixed “buy now” price, a broker has nothing to negotiate. You would just be paying commission on a number that was never going to change. Wix suggests skipping the broker when your budget is in the hundreds, not the thousands.

Here is a quick way to decide:

  • You can probably do it yourself if: the name costs under about $1,000, the owner is easy to find, or the domain is already listed for sale somewhere.
  • A broker may be worth it if: the price could reach five figures, the owner is a company that is hard to reach, or you need to stay anonymous so the price doesn’t jump when the owner learns who you are.

I work as a domain broker myself, so I’ll say it plainly: for a name in the hundreds of dollars, most people can do this on their own.

Step 1: Find out what kind of “taken” it is

Not all taken domains are the same. Type the name into your browser and see what happens. You will usually land in one of three situations:

  • A real, active website. The owner is using the name for a business. These are the hardest to buy, and often the most expensive. Be realistic.
  • A parked page or a “for sale” banner. This is the best case. Look for a price, a “make an offer” button or a marketplace link.
  • A blank page or an error. The name may be unused, forgotten or held as an investment. These owners are often open to offers.

Before you write to anyone, check the big marketplaces such as Afternic and Sedo. If a name is listed there, you can often buy it in a few clicks, and the marketplace handles the transfer. To see what a listing looks like, here are two of my own: Juronix.com on Sedo and RestInJoy.com on Afternic. You can read the thinking behind each in the Juronix case study and the RestInJoy case study.

Step 2: Find the owner

Start with a public lookup. The ICANN lookup tool shows registration details for most domains. Many owners hide their details behind a privacy service, so you may see the service’s name instead of a person. Don’t stop there. Try these:

  • Look for a contact page, a footer email or a “for sale” link on the website itself.
  • Check the site’s social media links and any LinkedIn page for the business.
  • Use the email or contact form in the lookup result. Many privacy services pass your message on to the real owner.
  • If it’s a company, try to reach the owner or a manager. GoDaddy warns that emails to a general inbox or a website admin usually get ignored.

Keep notes as you go: where you found each contact and when you wrote to them. It helps when you follow up.

Step 3: Work out a fair budget before you write

This is the step most beginners skip, and it’s where people overpay. Before you contact the owner, decide on two numbers: the price you would be happy with, and the highest price you will pay. Write both down.

Here is how to get a realistic idea of value:

  • Look at similar names. Check names of the same length and type on marketplaces. An asking price is not a selling price, and asking prices are often much higher than what people actually pay. I show how this works with real sales in Why This Domain Sits at $3,000–$4,500, Not $10,000.
  • Use an appraisal tool as a rough guide. GoDaddy suggests this for setting a budget. Treat the result as a starting point, because most tools lean on old sales data. I wrote about that limit in my CircleID article on how AI is changing what makes a domain brandable.
  • Ask what the name is worth to you. A name that saves you thousands in marketing is worth more to you than to a stranger. But don’t let excitement push you past your maximum.
  • Have a backup name ready. When you can walk away happily, you negotiate better.

If the asking price is far above anything you can justify, it’s fine to walk away. There will be other names.

Step 4: Write a short, friendly first email

The best first message is short, polite and calm. You’re not begging, and you’re not trying to trick anyone. A few things help:

  • Write from a personal email address, not a company one, so it doesn’t look like a big business is asking.
  • Leave out your big plans and your funding. The more the owner senses you need the name, the higher the price can climb.
  • Don’t pretend to be someone else.
  • Let the owner name a price first. GoDaddy recommends this. If they ask for your number, give a reasonable offer based on your research.

Here is a simple template you can copy and change:

Subject: Question about yourdomain.com

Hi [Name],

I came across yourdomain.com and I’m interested in it for a small project I’m building. Is it something you would consider selling?

If so, could you share a price you’d be comfortable with? Thanks for your time either way.

[Your name]

Short messages are easier to answer, so keep it that way.

Step 5: Negotiate calmly and slowly

Some owners reply in a few days. Some take weeks. Some never answer. If a week passes with no reply, send one polite follow-up. After that, wait or move on. Even a “no” isn’t always final. GoDaddy suggests checking back later, because an owner’s situation can change.

When an owner replies with a price, don’t accept it straight away, and don’t be rude about it either. Make a fair counteroffer that is clearly below their price but not insulting, and leave room to meet in the middle.

A few habits that help:

  • Move in small steps. Big jumps tell the owner you have a lot of room.
  • Stay friendly. People sell to people they like.
  • Never go above your maximum. If you do, you were negotiating with your feelings, not your budget.
  • If the owner offers a payment plan, read the terms carefully first. It can make a pricey name affordable, but make sure you know who owns the domain until you finish paying.

Step 6: Protect your money and your name

Once you agree on a price, this is the part that matters most. Never send money directly to a stranger.

Use an escrow service. A service like Escrow.com holds your payment until the domain is confirmed in your account. The seller knows the money is real, and you know you won’t pay for nothing. If a seller refuses to use escrow, GoDaddy’s advice is to walk away.

Ask for proof of ownership. The seller should be able to show they really own the domain, for example with a purchase receipt. This protects you from buying a stolen domain.

Get the details in writing. Confirm the domain name, the price and how the transfer will work, in an email or a short agreement.

Check for trademark trouble. If a name copies someone’s registered brand, the brand owner can start a dispute, and you could end up having to give the domain up. GoDaddy lists trademark conflicts as a reason to walk away. Search the name in the USPTO trademark database (or your own country’s database) before you pay. I explain the whole check in plain English in How to Check If a Domain Is Trademarked Before You Buy.

Confirm the transfer. After the transfer, check that the domain is in your account, turn on privacy protection if you want it, and switch on two-factor login.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Paying before there is a safe process. No escrow, no payment.
  • Falling in love with one name. Emotional buyers overpay.
  • Writing long, desperate emails. Short and calm works better.
  • Skipping the trademark check. It’s quick to do and expensive to ignore.
  • Trusting one appraisal number. Use it as a hint, not a fact.
  • Giving up after one email. One polite follow-up is normal.

When hiring a broker is the right call

Doing it yourself isn’t always the best answer. Think about a broker if the name is worth a lot to your business, the price could reach five figures, the owner is hard to reach, or you need to stay anonymous.

If you go that way, ask how the broker is paid. A percentage fee earns the broker more when the price is higher, so ask whether a flat fee is possible, how long they will keep trying, and what happens to your money if the owner says no. Give them your maximum price and a clear walk-away point, too.

Final thoughts

Buying a taken domain on a small budget is mostly about being organized and patient. Find out what kind of “taken” you’re dealing with, find the owner, set your numbers before you write, keep the first email short, and always use escrow.

You won’t win every time, and that’s fine. Some owners won’t reply, and some will ask for too much. But for a name in the hundreds of dollars, you now have what you need to try, without paying commission to anyone.

Frequently asked questions

Can I buy a domain without a broker?

Yes. If the domain is free, you can register it at any registrar. If it is already taken, you can contact the owner directly, agree on a price in writing and use escrow to move the domain safely.

How much should I offer for a domain name?

There is no fixed rule. Look at similar names, use an appraisal tool as a rough guide and decide your maximum before you write. GoDaddy suggests letting the owner name a price first, then answering with a fair offer below it.

Is it safe to buy a domain from a stranger?

It can be, if you use escrow, ask for proof of ownership, get the terms in writing and check for trademark problems first. Never send money straight to someone you don’t know.

Do I need escrow for a small purchase?

Yes. It protects both sides, even on small deals. GoDaddy’s advice is to walk away from any seller who refuses to use escrow.

How long does it take to buy a taken domain?

Plan for a month or more. GoDaddy says 4 to 8 weeks from first contact to transfer is typical, because finding the owner, negotiating and moving the domain each take time.

Do I own a domain forever once I buy it?

Not exactly. You own the registration, but you have to renew it, usually every year. Turn on auto-renew so you don’t lose the name by accident.
