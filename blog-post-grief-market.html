The Grief Market Nobody Likes to Talk About

And three domains that fit right into it. Published as a LinkedIn newsletter by Imteaz Chowdhury.

Imteaz (Galib) Chowdhury
Domain Broker & Investor · October 6, 2026

Nobody wakes up excited to work on grief. But a lot of smart people are quietly building in this space, and after digging into the numbers, I understand why.

I own three .com domains that fit this market well (RestInJoy.com, GriefPlatform.com and Losswell.com), so I spent some time looking at the data. Here's what I found.

How big is the need?

In 2025, 3,094,593 people died in the United States, according to the CDC's provisional data. That's one country in one year.

Behind every one of those deaths are people left behind. Here's a rough example, and it's my own assumption, not an official statistic: if each death leaves three adults truly grieving, that's about 9 million newly bereaved Americans every year.

Most people find a way through grief, but not everyone. A meta-analysis found that about 9.8% of bereaved adults are at risk of prolonged grief disorder, so roughly one in ten. After unexpected losses, like accidents, suicides or disasters, another meta-analysis found the rate was close to 49%.

And the money?

Market estimates change a lot depending on what each research firm counts, so please read these as rough ranges.

  • The grief counseling market is estimated at $4.03 billion in 2026. It's projected to reach $5.83 billion by 2030, which is about 9.7% growth a year.
  • One report puts the global funeral services market at around $80 billion in 2026. Other reports for the wider death-care industry say $70.4 billion (2025) or $149 billion (2026).
  • QR-code memorials, the kind you scan at a grave, are valued at around $450 million, and are projected to reach $1.2 billion by 2033.

The exact number matters less than the direction. Every estimate points up.

The part that surprised me: work

Grief doesn't stay at home when you go back to work.

About 80% of US employers have a bereavement policy, but the average leave is only around 5.5 days. Grief professionals recommend much more.

A survey from late 2025 found that nearly half of employees got two days or less of paid leave, and one in four got none. Nearly half said their work performance dropped afterward, and over 70% said it hit their mental health.

Empathy's research says grief can affect work for up to 18 months, and settling a loved one's affairs can take up to 20 months. In the UK, Sue Ryder estimates bereavement costs the economy about £23 billion a year.

So we have a five-day policy for an 18-month problem. Gaps like that are where new companies usually start.

What's changing

  • Funeral homes went digital fast. In 2023, 60.5% of US funeral homes offered livestreaming, up from 21.5% in 2019.
  • Memorials are moving online. One industry source reports digital memorial platform usage rose 33% in a year, and expects cremation to reach 78% by the end of 2026. Those are vendor-reported figures, so treat them as a direction, not a fact.
  • Support is going remote. Research suggests online tools make grief support easier to reach, especially for people living far away or unable to leave home.

The three domains

A domain is not a business. It's a head start on one. Here's how I see each of mine.

RestInJoy.com

A gentle twist on "Rest in Peace," for a world that increasingly says "celebrate a life" instead of "mourn a death." It's short, warm, and easy to say out loud, even at a funeral home. It could suit celebration-of-life services, tribute and memorial platforms, funeral-tech startups, pet memorial brands, or a QR-code memorial product.

GriefPlatform.com

This one says exactly what it is, and in a sensitive field, clear names build trust quickly. It suits grief-support apps, online peer communities, counseling marketplaces, and companies that provide bereavement benefits to employers. One idea: a marketplace that connects grief coaches and therapists with people who need them. Another: a portal that helps HR teams support staff after a loss.

Losswell.com

Soft, modern, and not clinical, in the style of today's wellness brands. That matters for a product someone might open at 2 a.m. It could suit a consumer grief app, a guided journaling subscription, a care-package brand, or a podcast or newsletter about healthy grieving.

Who could use these, and how

  • Grief-tech or mental-health startup: Losswell or GriefPlatform. Put up a landing page with a waitlist before you build anything.
  • Funeral home or death-care group: RestInJoy. Offer families a branded memorial and livestream page.
  • HR-benefits or EAP provider: GriefPlatform. Package a "support after loss" program for employers.
  • Therapist, coach or nonprofit: GriefPlatform or Losswell. Start a directory, a group program, or a paid community.
  • Pet-loss or memorial-product brand: RestInJoy. Tribute pages and QR memorial products fit well.
  • Writer or creator: Losswell. Build a newsletter, podcast, or book brand around it.

If you're thinking about building something, a simple order works:

  1. Pick one audience (families, employers or practitioners), not all three.
  2. Talk to about ten people before you write any code.
  3. Launch a one-page site with a clear promise and an email sign-up.
  4. Partner with one existing group, like a funeral home or a support circle.
  5. Add the product only after people start asking for it.

A few honest notes

Market numbers vary a lot between research firms, and some come from commercial reports and vendor blogs, so they're estimates. This is also a sensitive field. Anything built here should be made with care, with qualified professionals involved where support is clinical, and with clear links to crisis help. And nothing here is medical advice.
