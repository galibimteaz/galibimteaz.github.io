How to Check If a Business Name Is Available (Beyond the Domain)

You found a name you love, and the .com is free. Don’t buy anything yet. A free domain only means nobody registered that web address. It doesn’t tell you whether a company down the street already trades under the name, or whether a trademark owner could force you to drop it a year from now.

Short answer: To check if a business name is available, search five places: your state’s business registry, the USPTO trademark database, county or state DBA records, a plain web search, and the domain plus social handles. If you plan to sell abroad, add WIPO’s Global Brand Database. All of them are free to search. Go in the order below. The quick checks knock out weak names early, so you don’t waste time on the slow ones.

I know this from the domain side of the business. After I bought a name, a company emailed me about its trademark, and I wrote about it in my domain investing mistakes. This is the checking routine I wish I had followed first. It uses only free tools, and you should plan for about an hour per name.

What “available” really means

There’s no single national database for business names in the US, so a name can pass one check and fail the next. The U.S. Small Business Administration (SBA) lists four separate ways a name gets registered or protected. Each has its own register.

TypeWhat it doesWhere you check
Entity name (LLC, corporation)Protects the name at state levelYour state’s Secretary of State business search
TrademarkProtects the name at federal levelUSPTO Trademark Search
DBA (“doing business as”)No legal protection by itself, but may be requiredCounty, city or state DBA records
Domain nameProtects your website addressAny domain registrar’s search

So the real question isn’t “is the name taken?” It is “is the name free in every place that matters to my business?” The steps below cover each place.

Step 1: Run a quick web search

This takes five minutes and kills weak names fast. Search the exact name in quotes, then try the name with your industry, the name with “LLC” or “Inc”, and a few spelling variations. Say the name out loud and search for anything that sounds the same. For a name like Bluefern, that means “Bluefern”, “Blue Fern” and “Blufern”, each with and without your industry word.

What you want to know is simple: does anyone in my field already use this? Names that were never registered won’t appear in the official databases below, but they often appear here. If you find a company in your industry with the same or a near-identical name, I’d walk away right now. There’s no point checking further.

Step 2: Search your state’s business registry

Planning an LLC or a corporation? Search the business entity database in the state where you’ll register. It’s usually run by the Secretary of State, and each state has its own rules for allowed words and company endings, so use your state’s official site.

The SBA notes that once a name is registered, other businesses in the same state can’t register the same one. That helps, but don’t lean on it too much. It only covers that one state, and it says nothing about trademarks.

Also try a plural or an added word, to see what is already on the register.

Step 3: Check DBA and county records

Sole proprietors and partnerships often trade under a DBA (also called a trade name, fictitious name or assumed name). Depending on where you live, these are filed with the state, county or city. Search the records for your area.

Here’s the catch. The SBA says several businesses can use the same DBA in one state, so a DBA is easy to get but gives you no legal protection. That makes this search useful for one thing only: seeing who is already using the name. It doesn’t prove the name is free.

Step 4: Search the USPTO trademark database

Most people skip this step, and it’s the one that can hurt the most. A name can be free in your state and still infringe someone else’s trademark. The USPTO recommends a clearance search before you apply, to see whether someone has already registered the same mark or a similar one.

The free tool is the USPTO Trademark Search at tmsearch.uspto.gov. It replaced the old TESS system, which was retired on November 30, 2023. It covers active and inactive trademarks as well as pending applications. A few tips for using it:

  • Search the exact name first, then spelling variations and words that sound alike.
  • Search each important word on its own if the full name gives few results.
  • Read the goods and services listed for any close match. A similar name used in a very different field may be less of a problem, but this is where a trademark attorney earns their fee.
  • Look at the status. Live registrations and pending applications are the ones to worry about. A dead or abandoned entry is less of a risk, but still worth reading.

Keep in mind that unregistered marks won’t appear here, even though they can still block you. That’s why the web search comes first. If you want to go deeper, here is my guide on how to check a domain for trademark problems.

Step 5: Check the domain and social handles

Now check the .com and any close alternatives. The SBA points out that your domain doesn’t have to match your legal business name. Even so, a name where the domain and the handles all match is easier to remember.

If the .com is taken, don’t stop at “unavailable.” Look at what is there:

  • An active business in your field: pick another name.
  • An unrelated site: the name may still work, but plan for the risk of confusion.
  • A parked page or a “for sale” notice: you may be able to buy it. See my guide on how to buy a taken domain without a broker.

Then check the social media platforms your customers use. Matching handles on LinkedIn, Instagram, X, YouTube or TikTok save you from awkward names like “yourname_official” later.

Step 6: Selling or registering outside the US

If you plan to work in other countries, add these free checks:

  • WIPO Global Brand Database. It searches international (Madrid) marks and many national collections from one place. Search each key word separately if the full name returns few results.
  • TMview. A free search run by the EUIPO that pulls in data from many national trademark offices.
  • Australia: .com.au and .net.au domains may soon need to match a business name or an Australian trademark. I covered the proposed change on CircleID: auDA moves to tighten .com.au and .net.au eligibility. When I wrote it, timing was still pending, so check auDA for the current rules.
  • UK: Companies House rejects company names that are “the same as” or “too like” an existing one (GOV.UK rules). Check trade marks separately at gov.uk/search-for-trademark.

How to read what you find

Write every result in one simple table, one row per check. Then sort the name into one of three groups.

ResultWhat you foundWhat to do
GreenNo match anywhere, or only unrelated names in far-away fieldsGo ahead. Register the name and the domain quickly.
YellowSimilar names in a different field, or an active .com owned by someone elseSlow down. Read the goods and services, and ask a trademark attorney if the name matters a lot.
RedSame or similar name in your field, or a live trademark that covers your goods or servicesDrop it. A rebrand later costs far more than a new name now.

A made-up example. Say you’re checking “Bluefern Studio.” The state registry shows no exact match. USPTO shows a live “Blue Fern” mark, but for garden tools, and your business is design. The .com is a parked page. That is a yellow result: probably workable, but worth a professional opinion before you spend money on branding.

Do startup name checker tools work?

Many websites offer a one-box “name checker” that tests the domain and social handles together. They are handy for a first pass, because they save you from typing the same name into ten sites.

Here’s my take. I treat them as a first filter and nothing more. A tool can tell you a domain is free and still miss a live trademark, or skip your state’s registry altogether. So I test the result by hand on the official site before I decide anything.

What to do if the name is taken

  • Same name, same field: choose a different name. Fighting for it rarely pays off.
  • Similar name, different field: get advice before you commit. Whether customers could be confused is the key test.
  • Only the .com is taken: consider a clearly different name instead of a clumsy variation like adding “get” or “hq”. My post on why meaningless brand names built big companies explains why invented names can be easier to clear. I also wrote on CircleID about how AI answer engines are rewriting what makes a domain “brandable”, where distinctive names may matter more than exact-match keywords.
  • The owner may sell: make a fair offer, and use escrow for any domain purchase.

The checklist in one place

  • Search the web for the exact name, the name plus your industry, and sound-alikes.
  • Search your state’s business registry.
  • Check county or state DBA records.
  • Search USPTO Trademark Search, and WIPO or TMview if you work abroad.
  • Check the .com and the social handles.
  • Write the results down, sort them into green, yellow or red, and decide.

This article is general information, not legal advice. If the name is important to your business, a trademark attorney can read the results with you.

Frequently asked questions

How do I check if a business name is available for free?

Search four free places: your state’s business registry, the USPTO trademark database, county or state DBA records, and a plain web search. Then check the domain and social handles. All of these can be searched at no cost.

How do I check if a company name is available online?

In the US, use the Secretary of State business search for the state where you will register. Each state runs its own search, and there is no single national database. In the UK, the Companies House name check does the same job.

If the domain is available, does that mean the business name is available?

No. A free domain only means nobody has registered that web address. Another company can still use the name, and a trademark owner can still object to it.

Does registering my LLC name stop others from using it?

Only at state level. The SBA explains that an entity name protects you in your state, while a trademark protects you at federal level. A registered LLC name does not give you trademark rights.

Can two businesses have the same name?

Sometimes. A state will not register the same entity name twice. But several businesses can use the same DBA in one state, and trademark conflicts depend on whether customers could be confused.

Are startup name checker tools enough?

They are a good first filter for domains and social handles. They don’t replace the state registry or a trademark search, so confirm any result on the official site before you decide.
